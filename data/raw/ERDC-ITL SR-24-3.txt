Abstract 
The A/E/C Computer-Aided Design (CAD) Standard has been developed 
by the CAD/Building Information Modeling (BIM) Technology Center for 
Facilities, Infrastructure, and Environment to eliminate redundant CAD 
standardization efforts within DoD and the Federal Government. This 
manual is part of an initiative to develop a nonproprietary CAD standard 
that incorporates existing industry, national, and international standards 
and to develop data standards that address the entire life cycle of facilities 
within DoD. 
The material addressed in the A/E/C CAD Standard includes level/layer 
assignments, digital file naming, and standard symbology. The CAD/BIM 
Center’s primary goal is to develop a CAD standard that is generic enough 
to operate under various CAD software packages (such as Bentley’s 
MicroStation and Autodesk’s AutoCAD) while incorporating existing 
industry standards when possible. 
While this Standard encompasses many CAD concepts and practices, it is 
not intended to limit the capabilities of other advanced modeling software. 
Ultimately, a BIM / Civil Information Modeling standard will be developed 
to standardize the additional capabilities of other software.

1 Introduction 
1.1 Background 
The Architecture, Engineering, and Construction (A/E/C) CAD Standard 
has been developed by the Computer-Aided Design/Building Information 
Modeling (CAD/BIM) Technology Center for Facilities, Infrastructure, and 
Environment (hereafter referred to as the Center) to eliminate redundant 
standardization efforts within the DoD and the Federal Government. This 
manual is part of an initiative to develop a nonproprietary CAD standard 
that incorporates existing industry, national, and international standards. 
Additionally, this standard is intended to support the entire life cycle of 
facilities within the DoD. This report supersedes A/E/C CAD Standard, 
Release 6.1 (ERDC/ITL TR-19-7). 
The immediate benefits of CAD standards are the following:  
• Consistent CAD products for customers 
• Uniform requirements for architecture-engineering deliverables 
• Sharing of products and expertise 
Recognizing such potential benefits, each of the DoD agencies 
independently initiated efforts to establish CAD standards in the late 
1980s. In 1989, the Air Force Logistics Command released the 
Architectural and Engineering Services for Computer-Aided Design and 
Drafting (CADD) Implementation within Air Force Logistics Command. 
Headquarters, US Army Corps of Engineers (USACE), in 1990 published 
Engineer Manual 1110-1-1807, Standards Manual for U.S. Army Corps of 
Engineers Computer-Aided Design and Drafting (CADD) Systems. In 
1993, the Naval Facilities Engineering Command (NAVFAC) distributed 
its Policy and Procedures for Electronic Deliverables of Facilities 
Computer-Aided Design and Drafting (CADD) Systems. 
To consolidate these efforts into a single standard, the Center was tasked 
to develop standards for the A/E/C disciplines. This manual presents the 
Center’s continuing effort at standardizing CAD requirements for A/E/C 
design and construction documents.

1.2 United States National CAD Standard 
In 1995, the combined resources of the Center, the American Institute of 
Architects (AIA), the Construction Specifications Institute (CSI), the 
United States Coast Guard, the Sheet Metal and Air Conditioning 
Contractors National Association (SMACNA), the General Services 
Administration (GSA), and the National Institute of Building Sciences 
(NIBS) Facility Information Council began to develop a single CAD 
standard for the United States. Working together, these organizations 
agreed to develop an integrated set of documents that collectively would 
represent the United States National CAD Standard (NCS).

1.3 Objectives 
The objective of the A/E/C Standards (i.e., A/E/C CAD Standard and 
A/E/C Graphics Standard) is to aid in the documentation of design intent 
to be consistently, efficiently, and effectively transmitted through the

construction phase to operations and maintenance (O&M) and back to

1.4 Approach 
The approach chosen to respond to A/E/C community comments and 
feedback entailed reviewing their concerns and recommendations. If the 
comment was from a purely personal preference standpoint, it was 
disregarded. If the comment proposed an object opportunity for 
improvement, a workaround was determined on how to best resolve the 
issue while still maintaining the intent of industry standards. A Frequently 
Asked Questions (FAQ) page was established to document the interim 
solutions. This document updates the A/E/C Standards to incorporate 
those solutions. 
The intent of this document is to embrace the National CAD Standard 
while providing more guidance and clarifying areas of misunderstanding. 
This document will not cover every circumstance encountered in drawing 
production. There may be specialized instances where it will be up to the 
site to develop internal best practices. 
1.5 Scope 
This manual provides guidance and procedures for preparing CAD 
products within the DoD. Chapters 2–5 of this manual address topics such 
as presentation graphics, level/layer assignments, digital file naming, and 
standard symbology. Appendix A contains tables on model file level/layer 
names. 
1.6 Target Systems 
This standard does not target any specific CAD system or software but 
does specifically target CAD workflows. To ensure successful translations 
among CAD applications, certain system-specific characteristics were 
considered and the standard adjusted accordingly. During the preparation 
of the standard, certain baseline decisions were made: 
• The standard must be applicable to the latest release of commercially 
available CAD packages. AutoCAD and MicroStation were chosen 
based on their prevalence within DoD.

• The standard is based on CAD applications that utilize level/layer 
names and external reference files. 
1.7 Design Applications and Other Applications 
Numerous design applications have been developed to run on top of or 
alongside basic CAD engines. These applications can be used by designers 
to generate graphics inside CAD files.  
Document management systems that contain attributes or metadata for 
individual files and have such features as attribute exchange are becoming 
standard tools for management of digital files. Use of these systems to 
store searchable metadata for files is encouraged. 
1.8 Coordination with Design Agent 
With all the complexity and options currently available, it becomes vitally 
important to coordinate the fundamental aspects of design. Each project 
should have an initial discussion and agreement between the designer and 
customer on topics such as: design software, review software, output file 
format(s), and so on.  
1.9 Additions/Revisions 
This Standard is intended to be neither static nor all inclusive. Suggestions 
for improvements are strongly encouraged so that subsequent updates will 
reflect the input and needs of the users.

2 CAD File Organization 
2.1 CAD File settings 
2.1.1 Available Design Area 
The two most extensively used CAD applications within the DoD, 
AutoCAD and MicroStation, both provide for a design area with nearly 
infinite range in each positive and negative axis (x,y,z). 
2.1.2 File Accuracy (Units) 
CAD systems allow the designer to work in real-world units. The most 
common units are feet:inches, survey feet:hundredths of survey feet (or 
thousandths), and meters:millimeters. 
2.1.3 International Feet versus Survey Feet 
Many sites must contend with the initial question as to whether a 
particular project is designed using international feet or survey feet. In 
some states, it is specified by statute that units of measure for grid 
coordinates must be either international feet or survey feet. The two units 
are defined as follows: 
• International feet: 1 ft = 0.3048000 m. 
• US survey feet (SF): 1 SF = 1,200/3,937 m. 
Note: At the time of the revision of this document, the US survey foot is 
scheduled to be deprecated by the National Institute of Standards and 
Technology (NIST) and the National Geodetic Survey (NGS). Be sure to 
check with the issuing agency for required coordinate systems and units.  
Note: If a drawing has already been created using international feet, 
changing the master units to survey feet will not automatically scale all 
elements in the drawing to survey feet. Also, survey feet and 
international feet should not be mixed in similar disciplines due to 
horizontal shifts over large distances. 
2.1.4 Origin (Global Origin) 
Positioned within every digital drawing file is an origin (“global origin” in 
MicroStation and “origin” in AutoCAD). The origin of a drawing file is 
ERDC/ITL SR-24-3 
6 
important because it serves as the point of reference from which all other 
elements are located. Origins are typically defined in a drawing file by the 
Cartesian coordinate system of x, y, and z. 
The benefit of standardizing the location of the origin of a drawing is most 
notable in the use of external reference files (see Section 2.3.1.1). A 
standardized origin is also helpful when translating files between CAD 
applications. The recommended global origin for files in both AutoCAD 
and MicroStation drawings is x = 0, y = 0, and z = 0.  
2.2 CAD File Contents 
CAD files contain various types of designs (2D or 3D) used to generate 
construction plans. The contents of these files will vary depending on the 
design authoring software but typically contain the following types of 
areas: 3D modeling, 2D drawing, and/or the 2D sheets (see Sections 2.2.1, 
2.2.2, and 2.2.3). Depending on project requirements, project size, or 
design authoring software, the CAD files for a project may be organized by 
file use (e.g., project, discipline, modeling/drawing/sheet area type, file 
extension, etc.) and contain one or multiple types of areas (Figures 2-1 and 
2-2).  
Note: A MicroStation design file (.dgn) may have multiple modeling 
areas (design models) and multiple drawing areas (drawing models), 
while an AutoCAD design file (.dwg) will have only one modeling area or 
drawing area (model SPACE). Both MicroStation and AutoCAD may 
contain multiple sheet areas (MicroStation:Sheet Models, 
AutoCAD:Layouts/Paper Spaces).

2.2.1 Modeling Area 
The modeling area is where the 3D representation of physical components 
of a design (e.g., terrains, roads, curbs, columns, walls, windows, 
ductwork, piping, etc.) are drawn at full size (see Figure 2-2). Annotation 
is typically used sparingly in the modeling area to avoid text being 
misinterpreted as drawing content in alternate views. 
2.2.2 Drawing Area 
The drawing area is where the 2D graphical representation of physical 
components of a design are drawn at full size along with any annotation 
items (see Figures 2-1 and 2-2). Drawing areas can be generated either by 
placing graphics or by cutting views from modeling areas. Drawing areas 
include items such as plans, sections, elevations, and details. 
2.2.3 Sheet Area 
The sheet area is where views of the modeling areas and/or drawing areas 
are assembled to create a digital representation of the full-size plotted 
sheet. The sheet area contains a reference to the border sheet at 1:1. The 
addition of sheet-specific information (e.g., sheet annotation, symbols, 
sheet notes, etc.) completes the construction of the sheet area (see Figures 
2-1 and 2-2). 
2.3 CAD File Types 
A CAD file is a container of the areas documented above. The CAD file 
types are model files and sheet files. It is important to name files 
appropriately so that model and sheet files can be easily distinguished. 
2.3.1 Model Files 
Model files contain modeling areas and/or drawing areas. Model files are 
developed or assembled prior to creation of sheet files. The components 
contained within a model file may be referenced by other models. Each 
reference shall have a unique name or logical description describing what is 
being referenced. 
Referencing digital drawing information makes changes readily apparent 
to all design disciplines. This real-time access to the work of others 
ERDC/ITL SR-24-3 
10 
ensures accuracy and consistency within a set of drawings and helps 
promote concurrent design efforts.  
Note: Never bind/merge external references into the sheet areas since 
that will duplicate linework and increase the amount of work required to 
make future revisions. 
2.3.1.1  Border Sheet Model Files 
Border sheets are drawing areas in model files externally referenced by all 
disciplines to create sheet areas. This model file contains border linework, 
the title block, and project-specific symbols and text. All disciplines shall 
use the same border sheet within a project (for more information on 
border sheets, see the A/E/C Graphics Standard). 
2.3.1.2  Raster References 
Although raster imagery is not accurate, it allows for the reader to relate 
the design linework to real world objects. For interoperability, the most 
common raster file types referenced are TIF, JPG, and PDF. Raster file 
names shall conform to the model file naming convention with a model file 
type of “IG” (e.g., (Project Code)C-IG(User Defined).tif/jpg/pdf). 
Raster File Type uses: 
• GeoReferenced TIF: Best for aerial photographic imagery, maps, and 
drawings where the image is referenced under linework to add clarity. 
• JPG: Best for nonaerial photographic imagery used for depicting 
existing conditions referenced to sheets. 
• PDF: Best for historic drawings used as background images. 
Note: It is suggested that MrSID files are not directly referenced. The 
files are extremely large and unwieldy to manage in a CAD/BIM / civil 
information modeling (CIM) environment. It is recommended that the 
MrSID image be clipped, resampled, and saved as a georeferenced TIF 
file. The smaller file size allows the software to open faster and plot more 
efficiently. 
2.3.2 Sheet Files 
A sheet file is a file that contains a sheet area or multiple sheet areas. A 
sheet file is a ready-to-plot CAD file. Sheet files may also contain drawing

11 
area(s) that have annotation that is appropriate for the sheet area that is 
referencing it. 
2.4 CAD File Naming Conventions 
Naming conventions for CAD files allow users to determine the contents of 
a file without opening it. They also provide a convenient and clear 
structure for organizing CAD files within a project directory.  
Spaces are not allowed in any CAD filename. An underscore (_) or 
CamelCase may be used in lieu of a space, or a hyphen (-) may be used as a 
separator. 
2.4.1 Project Code 
The CAD file naming conventions require a Project Code (1 to 20 
characters) at the beginning of the filename. The Project Code shall be 
identified at the start of each project to ensure consistent filenames within 
that project. Some examples of Project Codes include: 
• the official agency project number and 
• the project number defined by the contract issuing agency CAD 
manager for their record system. 
The purpose of a Project Code is to indicate which project a CAD file is 
associated with. 
When deciding on a standard file naming convention, the user might 
consider the use of a special character such as an underscore (_) (as part 
of the 20-character Project Code) at the beginning of the model filename 
so that model files are grouped together.  
When a project includes multiple structures, features, or options, it is 
important to identify each file with the appropriate feature. This should be 
done as a part of the Project Code. For example, a model file for project 
P123 and building 2 could use a Project Code of _P123-Bldg2.

2.4.3 Model File Naming 
There are two different types of model files: modeling area files and 
drawing area files. The primary difference between these two types is that 
modeling areas are typically used to portray 3D and drawing areas are 
used to portray 2D. The NCS’s model file naming convention primarily 
focuses on 2D drawing practices and does not provide guidance for 
modeling area file types.

2.4.3.1  Modeling Area File Naming Convention 
The modeling area file naming convention (Figure 2-3) has three 
mandatory fields, and the fourth is optional. All fields must be in the 
correct sequence. 
Following the Project Code field, the first two-character field represents 
the Discipline Designator. The allowable characters for the Discipline 
Designator are listed in Table 2-1. The second character of the Discipline 
Designator field is typically a hyphen (-), but files may contain multiple 
discipline models so the second character may continue listing disciplines 
located within the file. If there are more than two disciplines, continue 
utilizing the main feature field. The next 8-character (maximum) field 
represents the main modeling feature of the file (e.g., wall, bracing, 
corridor, etc.). The final optional user-definable 15-character (maximum) 
field is available to further describe the file contents.  
It is recommended that the model area file naming schema be documented 
and be consistent for each project.

2.4.4 Sheet File Naming Convention 
The sheet file naming convention (Figure 2-5) contains four mandatory 
fields, and the fifth is optional. All fields must be in the correct sequence. 
Following the project code, the next 2 characters are the Discipline 
Designator with either a hyphen or an alphabetical designator (Table 2-3). 
The next character is the Sheet Type Designator (Table 2-4) followed by a 
2-character Sheet Sequence Number (01–99). Sheet Sequence Numbers do 
not need to be sequential. This allows for future insertion of sheets during 
design. The final field is a 10-character (maximum) user-definable field. 
Figure 2-5. Sheet file naming convention. 
When multiple sheets are in the same file, the word “SET” may replace 
either the Sheet Sequence Number (for all sheets under a specific sheet 
type designator) or the Sheet Type Designator and Sheet Sequence 
Number (for all sheets under a specific Sheet Type Designator). 
Alternately, the first and last sheet in a file may be listed as part of the 
user-definable field. The sheet filename shall coincide with the sheet 
identifiers that it contains.

3 Graphic Concepts 
3.1 Presentation Graphics 
The first step in establishing an effective CAD standard is the development 
of a uniform approach to presentation graphics. Presentation graphics 
typically consist of drawing elements such as lines, arcs, shapes, text, and 
their attributes (line color, line width, line style). This chapter presents 
brief overviews of the characteristics of presentation graphics and the 
philosophy used to standardize them. For more definition, see the A/E/C 
Graphics Standard.

3.1.4 Plotting 
Plotted documents are controlled by various files within the CAD 
application (pen tables, plot drivers, design scripts, feature tables, etc.). 
These files set the display of objects (i.e., line thickness, color, etc.) within 
the plotted document. Agencies should ensure that consistent drawings are 
produced from a digital file regardless of the type of printer or plotter used. 
Each agency may develop plot settings (derived from the A/E/C Standards) 
needed to convey the design intent (i.e., screening, color plotting, etc.). 
Note: For consistency/interoperability, it is recommended that plots first 
be sent to a common format, such as PDF, before paper plotting. 
3.1.5 Screening/Dithering 
Screening is the varying of the intensity of grayscale linework. Dithering is 
the process in which the density and pattern of black and white dots are 
varied to simulate different shades of gray. Both allow users to distinguish 
different aspects of a drawing when it is plotted.

assigned with a set screening percentage. When plotted, those items will 
be displayed at a lighter shade compared to items slated for demolition.

3.2 Text 
Since projects designed in CAD are planned for use many years into the 
future and files will be used by many different individuals, use of any 
nonstandard or proprietary fonts is not allowed. This includes fonts for 
symbology, logos, business titles, and so on. To improve the direct 
translation of fonts between applications, only Arial TrueType font is 
allowed in the construction drawing set (for more information on text, see 
the A/E/C Graphics Standard).

4.1 Level/Layer naming convention 
CAD levels or layers are analogous to overlays in manual drafting systems 
and serve to separate graphic elements (lines, shapes, text) according to 
the design discipline they represent (Figure 4-1). 
Figure 4-1. Typical levels/layers contained in a sheet file. 
The level/layer is the basic tool used in CAD for managing graphic 
information (Figure 4-2). The levels/layers defined within this standard 
are based on the recommendations set forth in “AIA CAD Layer 
Guidelines” (NIBS 2014). 
ERDC/ITL SR-24-3 
33 
Figure 4-2. Level/layer naming format. 
A basic level/layer name consists of a two-character Discipline Designator 
where the second character must be a hyphen (e.g., A- for Architectural, E- 
for Electrical), a four-character Major Group (e.g., A-DOOR for Doors, E
LITE for Lighting), and a four-character Minor Group (e.g., A-WALL
CNTR for wall center lines, E-LITE-CLNG for ceiling lights). For further 
differentiation, another four-character Minor Group may be used (e.g., 
A-WALL-FULL-EXTR for exterior full-height walls versus A-WALL
FULL-INTR for interior full-height walls). An optional item to indicate 
Status or Phase can also be added to every level/layer name (see Status 
(Phase) levels/layers later in this chapter). Between all groups, a hyphen 
must be placed. 
Since BIM/CIM processes capture most of the information that is defined 
in a CAD layer name, the Minor Groups may become redundant and 
unnecessary when those processes are used. 
The Appendix contains standard level/layer definitions. If additional 
layers/levels are needed, follow the format above and document the 
layer/level created with the project submittal as a variance.

5 Standard Symbology 
5.1 Introduction 
The symbology library contains the following four types of elements: Lines 
Types/Styles, Patterns, Symbols, and Objects. The use of such symbology 
enhances CAD productivity and provides an excellent opportunity for CAD 
standardization. 
Note: The A/E/C CAD Standard is following the lead of the U.S. National 
CAD Standard and incorporating Construction Specifications Institute’s 
MasterFormat numbers into the symbol names. This renaming of the 
symbols results in symbol libraries where symbols exist only once and 
are no longer limited to a particular discipline. 
5.2 Symbology Resources 
5.2.1 Cells/Blocks 
A cell in MicroStation and a block in AutoCAD are groups of graphical 
elements that can be manipulated as a single entity. Examples of typical 
cells/blocks are windows, doors, graphic scale keys, furniture, etc. Symbols 
are defined as cells/blocks that are representative of objects (e.g., electrical 
outlets, smoke detectors). Objects are defined as cells/blocks that are 
placed at the actual size of the item they are representing, no matter the 
scale of the drawing (e.g., 30 in. by 50 in. desk, 3 ft-0 in. door). Within the 
digital deliverables available as part of the A/E/C CAD Standard, the 
following symbology is provided: 
• MicroStation cells contained in cell libraries (.cel) 
• AutoCAD blocks contained in block libraries (.dwg files) with the 
block’s name and description combined into a single name. The block 
library only contains the definitions and not the graphics.  
5.2.2 Line Types/Styles 
Line types/styles are defined as a graphical representation of linear 
drawing features (e.g., utility lines, fence lines, contours). Linear patterns 
are defined as repeated drawing elements (e.g., lines, dots, circles) along a 
linear path. Line-style definitions determine the dash-dot sequence and 
ERDC/ITL SR-24-3 
37 
relative length of dashes, blank spaces, and the characteristics of any 
included text or shapes.  
5.3 Downloadable Resources 
Documentation and files for the entire symbology library are available for 
download from the Center’s website: https://cadbimcenter.erdc.dren.mil.

6 Conclusion 
Standards have been recognized as a vital tool in the development of CAD 
drawings for DoD. Without standards, CAD drawings would be difficult to 
review, interpret, organize, and archive, resulting in excessive time and 
monetary costs for DoD. With these factors in mind, the A/E/C CAD 
Standard has been an important document for DoD since the early 1990s 
by serving as the standard for creating CAD files.

14. ABSTRACT 
The A/E/C Computer-Aided Design (CAD) Standard has been developed by the CAD/Building Information Modeling (BIM) Technology 
Center for Facilities, Infrastructure, and Environment to eliminate redundant CAD standardization efforts within DoD and the Federal 
Government. This manual is part of an initiative to develop a nonproprietary CAD standard that incorporates existing industry, national, 
and international standards and to develop data standards that address the entire life cycle of facilities within DoD. 
 
The material addressed in the A/E/C CAD Standard includes level/layer assignments, digital file naming, and standard symbology. The 
CAD/BIM Center’s primary goal is to develop a CAD standard that is generic enough to operate under various CAD software packages 
(such as Bentley’s MicroStation and Autodesk’s AutoCAD) while incorporating existing industry standards when possible. 
 
While this Standard encompasses many CAD concepts and practices, it is not intended to limit the capabilities of other advanced modeling 
software. Ultimately, a BIM / Civil Information Modeling standard will be developed to standardize the additional capabilities of other 
software.
